Snake & Ladder Winning Strategy

How to win more games — openings, safe squares and dice maths · Last updated: 14 November 2026

Strategy guide

Snake & Ladder is a game of chance, but it is not a game of luck alone. Every square you land on is a decision: climb a ladder, dodge a snake, or sit still and keep your lead. This guide covers what actually changes the odds.

The best opening move

Your first roll matters more than any other. Landing on square 1 triggers the biggest ladder on the board and sends you to 38 in a single move — a 37-square head start that no other single square can match.

  • Roll 1 — instant jump to 38. The best possible start.
  • Roll 4, 5 or 6 — you land on 4, 5 or 6. Square 4 is a ladder base (to 14) and squares 5 and 6 are safe, so you set up a second ladder on your next turn.
  • Roll 2 or 3 — you land on 2 or 3, which are dead squares. Use this turn to plan rather than panic.
Rule of thumb: in the first few turns, prefer a roll that puts you on a ladder base over one that merely moves you forward.

Squares to avoid

Six snake heads sit on the board. Landing on one is a large, immediate loss, so treat these squares as danger zones and try to roll past them rather than onto them.

How to handle a 6

Rolling a 6 gives you a bonus turn — one more roll in the same turn. That sounds free, but there is a catch: three 6s in a row sends you back to square 1.

  • When you are behind, chase sixes. The extra roll is worth more than the risk of the reset, and climbing ladders is how you catch up.
  • When you are ahead, a non-six is often better. It breaks the six-streak counter without moving you, so you keep your lead and reset the risk.
  • Never chase a third six from a low square. Two sixes and a reset sends you all the way back and gifts your opponent a 90-square lead.

The end game: hitting exactly 100

You must land on exactly 100. If you are on 97 and roll 4, you stay on 97 and lose the turn. This makes the last stretch a delicate puzzle.

  • Work out the gap you need and wait for a roll that matches it exactly.
  • Stay off 93 and 95 — both are snake heads, and 95 in particular is a long slide right at the finish.
  • Remember that 78 climbs to 98, which is a superb final approach: two squares from home in one move.
Tip: sitting on 99 is uncomfortable but safe. From 99 you only need a 1, and no snake head can reach you.

The maths behind the dice

Each turn moves you an average of 3.5 squares before ladders and snakes are applied. With nine ladders worth 179 squares in total and six snakes worth 160, the board is close to neutral overall — which is why a good opening and smart bonus-six decisions genuinely matter rather than being decorative.

Because there are 15 jumps on a 100-square board, roughly one in seven rolls triggers a ladder or a snake. Position yourself so that the majority of those triggers work in your favour.

Frequently asked questions

Is there a guaranteed way to win Snake & Ladder?

No. Because every player shares the same dice and the same board, no outcome can be guaranteed. What you can control is your position: keep away from the snake heads, aim for ladder bases, and decide deliberately whether to spend a 6 on a bonus turn or bank it.

Should I always take the bonus turn when I roll a 6?

Not always. A bonus six is a free roll, but three sixes in a row sends you back to square 1. If you are safely parked on a high square, a non-six often keeps you ahead; if you are behind, chasing sixes is usually worth the risk.

What is the best opening move in Snake & Ladder?

Opening with a 1 lands you on the ladder at square 1 and shoots you straight to 38, which is the single biggest jump on the board. Any roll of 4, 5 or 6 also puts you on or near a ladder base, so early sixes are usually strong.

How many rolls does a typical game take?

Most games take roughly 40 to 70 rolls per player. The board has nine ladders and six snakes, so a fair share of rolls are spent climbing or sliding rather than moving.

Does playing the Daily Challenge improve my chance of winning?

It changes the maths rather than improving it. Every player in a daily challenge faces the identical dice sequence, so winning comes down purely to your decisions. It is a good way to practise openings without the randomness of a normal game.
